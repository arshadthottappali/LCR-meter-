  1. Connect (section 1 of the window): Interface VISA, resource GPIB0::1::INSTR, click Connect. The Instrument field shows HIOKI,3532,50,….
  2. Frequency sweep (section 2): for example 100 Hz to 1 MHz, 51 points, logarithmic.
  3. Test signal (section 3): for example level type CV, 1 V, speed NORMAL.
  4. Calibrate (section 4): click Calibrate fixture (open + short)… and follow the two pop-ups. No sample in the fixture during calibration.
  5. Measure (section 5): insert the sample, enter sample name, operator and test number, click Start sweep.
  6. The data is saved automatically as a CSV file in the data folder. Nothing else to do.

2 Safety first BEGINNER

The measurement terminals tolerate at most ±40 V DC (printed on the front panel: "UNKNOWN ±40 V DC MAX").

  • Discharge capacitors before connecting them (short the leads through a resistor of about 100 Ω–1 kΩ for a few seconds). A charged capacitor can destroy the meter's input.
  • Never measure live circuits or anything connected to mains or a power supply. Disconnect the component from its circuit first.
  • Do not measure batteries, charged supercapacitors or power supplies: their DC voltage and very low impedance are outside what the meter is made for.
  • Some samples (piezo elements, large inductors) can generate voltages when they are squeezed, heated or when current is interrupted. Handle them gently and let them settle.

4 What does an LCR meter measure? BEGINNER

An LCR meter applies a small AC (alternating) voltage of a chosen frequency to the sample, measures the AC current that flows, and compares the two. Two things are measured:

  • How big the current is for a given voltage. This gives the impedance magnitude |Z| in ohms (Ω), the AC version of resistance:
|Z| = Vrms / Irms     (unit: Ω)
  • How much the current is shifted in time relative to the voltage. This gives the phase angle θ in degrees.
θCurrent vs. voltageBehaves like
θ = 0°current in step with voltageresistor
θ = +90°current lags ¼ period behindinductor
θ = −90°current leads ¼ period aheadcapacitor
anything in betweena mixturereal components
Try it: phase shift and the impedance arrow

Left: voltage (blue) and current (orange) over two periods; the time shift is θ. Right: the same impedance as an arrow, split into resistance R (horizontal) and reactance X (vertical).

The 3532-50 reports exactly these two numbers, |Z| and θ. Every other quantity in this program (Cs, Ls, Rp, D, Q, …) is calculated from |Z|, θ and the frequency f, with the same formulas the meter uses internally.

Impedance as an arrow (complex number)

It is convenient to draw |Z| and θ as an arrow. Its horizontal part is the resistance R (energy is lost as heat); its vertical part is the reactance X (energy is stored and returned every cycle).

Z = R + jX
R = |Z|·cos θ    X = |Z|·sin θ    |Z| = √(R² + X²)
inductive : X > 0, θ > 0 (arrow points up)    capacitive : X < 0, θ < 0 (arrow points down)

Engineers write this as Z = R + jX, where j marks the "vertical" part (mathematically j = √−1). You do not need complex numbers to use the program; they only make the formulas short.

The frequency appears in almost every formula as the angular frequency:

ω = 2π·f     (unit: rad/s; f = 1 kHz → ω = 6283 rad/s)

6 Real components and self-resonance BEGINNER

Real parts are never ideal: every capacitor also has some resistance and inductance, every inductor some resistance and capacitance. These "parasitic" parts are what you usually want to measure.

Real capacitor

Real inductor

Self-resonance (SRF)

Because every part contains both an L and a C, there is always a frequency where their reactances cancel. This is the self-resonant frequency:

f₀ = 1 / ( 2π · √(L · C) )
  • Capacitor: below f₀ it behaves like a capacitor (θ ≈ −90°); at f₀ the phase crosses 0° and |Z| has its minimum, equal to the ESR; above f₀ it behaves like an inductor (θ > 0). Example: 100 nF with 30 nH of lead inductance → f₀ = 1/(2π√(30·10⁻⁹ · 100·10⁻⁹)) ≈ 2.9 MHz.
  • Inductor: below f₀ inductive; at f₀ |Z| has a maximum; above f₀ it behaves like a capacitor.

Practical meaning: a value such as "Cs = −39 nF at 5 MHz" or "Ls negative" is not an error. It only means that you are above (or below) the self-resonance, where that equivalent circuit no longer describes the part. Look at θ: as long as θ is close to −90° the capacitance values are meaningful.

7 Series and parallel equivalent circuits BEGINNER

From |Z| and θ the meter cannot know what is inside the part. It describes the part with the simplest circuit that gives the same |Z| and θ at that one frequency. There are two choices:

Both describe the same measurement; they are just two ways of writing it. They give (almost) the same C or L when the losses are small (D < 0.1), and very different values when the losses are large.

Which one should I use?

SituationUseReason
Low impedance (|Z| below about 100 Ω): large capacitors, small inductors, high frequencies Series (Cs, Ls, Rs)the series resistance (ESR, leads) dominates the losses
High impedance (|Z| above about 10 kΩ): small capacitors, large inductors, low frequencies Parallel (Cp, Lp, Rp)the parallel leakage / dielectric loss dominates
In betweenwhatever the datasheet / standard usesboth are correct; compare like with like
Dielectric and material samplesParallel (Cp, D)permittivity is defined from Cp

Converting between them

Cs = Cp · (1 + D²)      Cp = Cs / (1 + D²)
Ls = Lp · Q² / (1 + Q²)   Lp = Ls · (1 + 1/Q²)
Rp = Rs · (1 + Q²)       (Q = 1/D)

Example from a real measurement: a 100 nF ceramic capacitor at 1 MHz had D = 1.2, which gave Cs = 102 nF but Cp = 42 nF. Both are "right"; at D = 1.2 the part is no longer a good capacitor at that frequency, so a single C value is not very meaningful.

Why does Rp fall with frequency for a capacitor?

Because Rp is not a fixed resistor but a way of expressing the loss:

Rp = 1 / (ω · C · D)

Even with constant C and D, Rp falls by 10× for every 10× in frequency. This is normal. The DC leakage resistance (GΩ) can only be measured with an insulation tester at DC.

9 Connections: the 4-terminal method BEGINNER

The problem with two wires

If you connect a part with just two wires, the meter measures the part plus both wires:

The solution: separate wires for current and voltage (Kelvin connection)

  • H CUR (high, current): the meter's AC source pushes the test current out.
  • L CUR (low, current): the current comes back here and is measured.
  • H POT and L POT (potential): measure the voltage directly at the sample. They draw almost no current, so the resistance of their wires does not matter.
  • GUARD: connected to the meter's ground. It goes to the shield of the fixture or cables and catches stray currents that would otherwise flow around the sample (important for small pF capacitances and high MΩ impedances).

Rule: H CUR and H POT meet at one side of the sample, L CUR and L POT at the other side, as close to the sample as possible. On the usual 4-terminal fixture: red jacks = H side, blue jacks = L side, cyan/green jack = guard. Check the labels of your fixture.

Good cabling, step by step

  1. Use the shortest cables you can. Best is a fixture that plugs directly into the four BNC connectors.
  2. Twist or bundle H CUR with H POT, and L CUR with L POT, and run the two pairs side by side. Open loops act like antennas and add inductance (≈ 1 µH per metre of loose loop).
  3. Connect the GUARD to the fixture's guard jack (a BNC-to-banana adapter may be needed).
  4. Tape the cables down. If they move after calibration, the calibration is no longer accurate.
  5. Insert component leads so that the body is as close to the contacts as possible.
  6. Keep hands, metal tools and other cables away from the sample during the measurement.

How to see if your cabling is good: after calibrating, look at the SHORT values in the calibration file (or the log). A good short is a few tens of mΩ and some tens of nH. Long loose leads show up as about 0.2 Ω and 0.3 µH or more, and limit accuracy above about 100 kHz.

10 Fixture calibration (open / short) BEGINNER

Why calibrate?

Even with a 4-terminal connection, the fixture and the last few centimetres of wire add their own small impedance. Two effects matter:

  • Zs (series residual) adds to every reading. It matters for low impedances: large capacitors, small inductors, small resistors, and everything at high frequency.
  • Yo (stray admittance) is a small capacitance (typically 1–20 pF) across the sample. It matters for high impedances: small capacitors, large resistors, low frequencies.

How the app measures them

  1. OPEN: fixture empty → the meter sees only Zs + 1/Yo → gives Yo.
  2. SHORT: fixture terminals connected directly together → the meter sees only Zs.

Then every reading of the sample, Zm, is corrected point by point:

Zs   = Z_short
Yo   = 1 / (Z_open − Z_short)
Zdut = (Zm − Zs) / (1 − (Zm − Zs) · Yo)
Try it: what the fixture does to a capacitor, and what the correction gives back

Blue: the true Cs of the sample. Orange: what the meter reads without correction. Green dashed: after the open/short correction, calculated from simulated OPEN and SHORT readings. The default values are those of the long loose leads measured in the lab.

Doing it right

  • No sample in the fixture during either step.
  • OPEN: contacts apart, not touching anything; the same spacing the sample leads will have.
  • SHORT: a short, thick piece of wire (or the shorting bar) clamped at the same positions where the sample leads go. Do not just touch the clips together.
  • Use the same frequency range, number of points and test level that you will use for the measurement. The app does this automatically from sections 2 and 3, and warns you when they no longer match.
  • Calibrate again after moving cables, changing the fixture, or after a long time / large temperature change.
  • Use either this software calibration or the meter's front-panel OPEN/SHORT compensation, not both.

The calibration is saved as a JSON file in LCR_data\calibration\ and loaded again when the program starts. The CSV of every measurement records which calibration file was used.

Check: the column Z_raw_ohm holds the reading before correction. If raw and corrected values are almost the same, the fixture effects were negligible for that sample; if they differ a lot (typically above 1 MHz or for very small/large impedances), the calibration was essential.

Other settings

  • Averaging (meter): the meter averages N readings; noise drops by about √N, time rises N×. The 3532-50 accepts only 1 (off), 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 or 64, so the drop-down offers just these. 1 for normal work, 4–16 with SLOW2 for precise work.

  • Readings / point: the program measures each frequency N times and stores mean and standard deviation (Z_std, θ_std). Use 3–10 when you need error bars.
  • Trigger each point (*TRG): guarantees that every stored value is a fresh reading taken after the frequency change. Keep it on.

12 Frequency sweep settings BEGINNER

  • Range: 42 Hz … 5 MHz. Enter values with SI prefixes: 42, 120, 1k, 2.5k, 1M, 5M.
  • Logarithmic spacing (recommended): equal number of points per decade. Impedance changes by factors of 10, so this shows everything evenly.
  • Linear: equal steps in Hz. Useful only for a narrow range, e.g. around a resonance (1.0 – 1.2 MHz).
  • Custom list: exactly the frequencies you type, e.g. the standard test frequencies 100, 120, 1k, 10k, 100k, 1M.
  • Points: 11–21 for a quick look, 51 for normal work, 101–201 for smooth curves or sharp resonances.
  • The estimated time under the Start button is a rough guide; auto-ranging and the settling delay usually add 0.2–0.6 s per point.
  • Frequency resolution: the meter sets frequencies in steps of 0.1 Hz below 1 kHz, 1 Hz up to 10 kHz, 10 Hz up to 100 kHz, 100 Hz up to 1 MHz and 1 kHz above 1 MHz. The program rounds every frequency to these steps, so the frequency in the data file is exactly the one the meter used (e.g. a requested 53.06 Hz becomes 53.1 Hz). Close points that round to the same frequency are measured only once.

16 Accuracy and good practice BEGINNER

  • The meter is most accurate for medium impedances (roughly 10 Ω … 100 kΩ). Very small (< 1 Ω) and very large (> 10 MΩ) impedances, and frequencies above 1 MHz, are harder: use SLOW2, averaging, a good calibration and short, guarded cables.
  • Low-loss parts: the accuracy of D is limited (roughly ±0.001 under good conditions). A measured D of 0.0005 may really be anything from 0 to 0.0015.
  • Temperature changes many parts (class 2 ceramics by several % between 20 and 30 °C). Note the temperature in the Temperature field.
  • Class 2 ceramics age: their capacitance drops by about 1–2 % per decade of time after they were last heated (e.g. soldered). Results can differ from day to day.
  • Repeatability check: measure the same sample 3 times, removing and re-inserting it in between. The spread shows how much of the result is contact and handling, not the part.
  • Let the meter warm up for at least 30–60 minutes before precise measurements.

Traceability: can the result be trusted in a report?

A result is only traceable when the meter itself is calibrated (by a calibration lab, usually every year; see the sticker on the meter) and you can show which meter was used. The program helps:

  • Enter Meter ID (serial or inventory number from the meter's label) and Meter cal. due (date on the calibration sticker) in section 1. Both are written into every CSV header. The program warns before a sweep when the calibration date has passed.
  • Every CSV also records the instrument reply (*IDN?), all settings, the software version, the fixture calibration file used, the operator, date and time, and the raw readings before correction.
  • The fixture open/short calibration in section 4 is a correction of your cables and fixture, not a calibration of the meter. It does not replace the lab calibration.
  • For formal work (e.g. ISO/IEC 17025), also state the measurement uncertainty: start from the meter's accuracy (basic accuracy ±0.08 % for |Z| and ±0.05° for θ, larger at the edges of the ranges and above 1 MHz; see Hioki's accuracy tables), and add the repeatability you measured.

18 Advanced topics ADVANCED

18.1 Derivation of the parameters

With the measured phasor Z = |Z|·ejθ:

Z  = Rs + jX      Rs = |Z| cos θ,   X = |Z| sin θ
Y  = 1/Z = G + jB   G = cos θ / |Z|,  B = −sin θ / |Z|
X  = ωLs = −1/(ωCs)   →   Ls = X/ω,   Cs = −1/(ωX)
B  = ωCp = −1/(ωLp)   →   Cp = B/ω,   Lp = −1/(ωB)
D  = tan δ = |Rs/X| = |G/B| = |cot θ|,   Q = 1/D,   δ = 90° − |θ|

D is the same in both models because Rs/X and G/B are both equal to cot θ.

18.2 Open/short correction in detail

The fixture is modelled as a series impedance Zs followed by a shunt admittance Yo (an "L-network"):

Zm = Zs + 1 / (Yo + 1/Zdut)
SHORT (Zdut = 0):  Z_short = Zs
OPEN  (Zdut = ∞):  Z_open  = Zs + 1/Yo   →   Yo = 1/(Z_open − Z_short)
solve for Zdut:    Zdut = (Zm − Zs) / (1 − (Zm − Zs)·Yo)

The correction is applied to the complex mean of each point. If the OPEN reading overflowed at some frequency (typically below 100 Hz), Yo is taken as 0 there, which is correct because the stray admittance is then negligible. A third standard (LOAD correction with a known reference part) would also remove gain and phase errors of the cables; it is not implemented.

18.3 Dielectric and material measurements

For a flat sample of thickness d between parallel electrodes of area A:

C₀   = ε₀ · A / d        (empty-capacitor value; ε₀ = 8.854·10⁻¹² F/m)
ε′   = Cp / C₀ = Cp · d / (ε₀ · A)   (relative permittivity, real part)
ε″   = ε′ · D                    (loss part)
σ_ac = ω · ε₀ · ε″            (AC conductivity, S/m)
M*   = 1/ε*  (electric modulus: M′ = ε′/(ε′²+ε″²),  M″ = ε″/(ε′²+ε″²))
Try it: dielectric calculator

Circular electrodes: A = π·(diameter/2)². Edge fields are ignored (see the notes below).

  • Use Cp and D (parallel model), CV mode, SLOW2 and averaging.
  • Edge (fringe) fields make C larger than ε₀A/d predicts, especially when d is not much smaller than the electrode size. A guard-ring electrode or a correction for the edge capacitance improves accuracy.
  • Good contact matters: sputtered or painted electrodes are much better than pressure contacts.
  • A Debye relaxation shows up as a step in ε′, a peak in ε″ (and D) at f = 1/(2πτ), and a semicircle in the Nyquist or Cole-Cole (ε″ vs ε′) plot. Conduction shows as ε″ rising as 1/f towards low frequencies. The simulator has a "Dielectric sample (Debye relaxation)" model to practise with.

18.4 Impedance spectroscopy and equivalent-circuit fitting

The CSV contains everything needed to fit equivalent circuits in external software (e.g. Python impedance.py, ZView, EC-Lab): use freq_Hz, Rs_ohm (Z′) and X_ohm (Z″). For electrochemical systems, use a low CV level (10 mV) so that the response stays linear. This program applies no DC bias, so electrochemical cells are measured at their open-circuit state.

18.5 How the program talks to the meter

The program uses GPIB through the Keithley KUSB-488A adapter, either via VISA (resource GPIB0::1::INSTR) or by calling the adapter driver's GPIB DLL (gpib488.dll, ni4882.dll) directly. One sweep point is:

:FREQuency 1.00000E+03      set the frequency
(settling delay)
*TRG                        trigger one fresh measurement (external trigger mode)
:MEASure?                   →  1.2552E+07,-87.45      (|Z| in Ω, θ in degrees)

At the start of a sweep the program sends :HEADer OFF, :MEASure:ITEM 5,0 (return |Z| and θ), :RANGe:AUTO ON, speed, averaging, level and :TRIGger EXTernal; at the end :TRIGger INTernal returns the meter to free-running mode.

  • All command strings are in lcr_app\instrument\commands_3532_50.json (Tools → Open command profile). After editing, use Tools → Reload command profile.
  • Tools → Verify command profile on meter sends every command and reads the error register (*ESR?); a FAIL means the meter rejected that command.
  • The GPIB console tab sends any command by hand. Commands ending with ? are queries and show the answer. Tick "Log all GPIB traffic" to see every command the program sends.
  • The Simulator interface imitates a 3532-50 with selectable test components, for training without hardware.

18.6 Speed versus quality

Time per point ≈ settling delay + readings/point × (averaging × measuring time + GPIB and auto-range overhead). Auto-ranging adds the most when |Z| changes a lot between points. For long series of identical samples, a custom frequency list with only the frequencies you need is the fastest option.
